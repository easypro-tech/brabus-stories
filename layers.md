# Layers

He was my grandfather. I called him Papa.

Papa left for two years at a time. Africa. The Middle East. Asia. I knew these words before I knew geography.

He blew up the earth. He laid a charge. He listened for what would come back. The echo came back different. By that "different" he found oil.

---

She was my grandmother. I called her Mama.

Mama worked at the Academy of Sciences. A geologist. She read the strata. What had been pressure. What had been time. What had been water.

Sometimes she left with him.

On her desk there were boxes of cores. I opened the lids and touched the cylinders. The rock was cold, like someone else's hand.

- This is time, - Mama said, and tapped the pale band with her nail.

- And this?

- Water. It was here before.

- And this?

- A crack. Don't touch.

---

They raised me.

Papa often sat down at the table and drew on a sheet of paper: a point, a line, another point. Mama watched and said:

- You're setting the charge too close to the water.

He didn't argue. He redrew it.

I sat across from them. I thought it was work.

---

I'm forty-six. I build encryption systems.

At night I'm alone. A notebook in front of me. I send a signal into the noise. I listen for what comes back.

Sometimes nothing comes back until morning. I just sit. In the morning I start over.

---

For thirty years I thought I had chosen it myself.

Then Papa was gone. Then Mama.

The boxes were in the closet, on the bottom shelf. I took them down one by one.

In one - cores, each wrapped in newspaper. On the newspaper - depth, pressure, water. I unwrapped one. The rock was the same. Cold.

In the other - Papa's notebooks. Charge. Time. Echo. The pencil had dug in. On the back - a trace. In the margin of one page: "Not that. Check." And an arrow.

I closed the notebook.

---

I sent a signal into the noise.

I waited.

It came back different.

I opened mine. I put an arrow.
